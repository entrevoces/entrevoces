[AUDIO MYSTERY: El ascensor de los tal vez. Opening sound: an elevator arrives on a floor that has no number. A restrained thriller soundtrack hums beneath dialogue.]

NARRATOR: Elena enters an empty office building carrying a file with four handwritten words: before, after, maybe and never. The elevator doors open without anyone pressing a button. Inside is a brass plaque: The Floor You Heard.

ELENA: Is this a dream?

VOICE ON INTERCOM: Only if you are sure.

NARRATOR: The intercom tells her that a witness may have seen a red truck. The voice has a warm accent but no identifiable source. Elena asks, “May have seen, or did see?” The floor indicator flickers between 5 and 15.

VOICE ON INTERCOM: Choose the sentence that you actually heard, not the one that feels complete.

[First choice: preserve “might have seen” or announce the person saw it with certainty. Both change the elevator's path. A careful listener can return and compare.]

NARRATOR: On the next floor, a clinician says a fictional patient has not had a symptom today. In one recording a tiny negation vanishes. The elevator doors are different colors, but the player must rely on sound; no icon gives away the answer.

ELENA: Not having a symptom and having a symptom are not the same story.

VOICE ON INTERCOM: True. But does the interpreter know enough to replace what was said?

ELENA: No. If the words are unclear, the interpreter should disclose the difficulty and request clarification.

[Second choice: admit hearing difficulty or invent a plausible answer. The hall beyond each opens onto a different recorded memory.]

NARRATOR: A third voice, which Elena recognizes as her own, says, “A hearing is scheduled for Tuesday.” Then another version announces the judge has already made a decision. The building shakes as if certainty were a weight on its beams.

ELENA: A scheduled proceeding isn't the same as a ruling. Stop the recording.

[Third choice: mark the procedural difference or follow a misleading summary. No gory outcome; the world simply becomes less reliable.]

VOICE ON INTERCOM: You made the building more honest. That does not mean you decided what happened.

ELENA: I only kept what was actually said.

[Endings: the elevator arrives at a quiet library where the sentences can be heard in their original forms, or loops back to the same floor with a new invitation to listen again. None of the fictional consequences count as evidence of real legal knowledge.]

NARRATOR: In the building that remembers every word, the only way out is to refuse the words you never heard.

[Afterword — The elevator returns in the listener's imagination. The narrator names the four doors without revealing which option to choose. Elena hears a grandmother say in Spanish, “Tal vez no,” and this time the building waits rather than filling in the silence.]

ELENA: A pause may be uncomfortable, but a false certainty can reshape what follows.

VOICE ON INTERCOM: What if you don't know which word you heard?

ELENA: Then I ask for it again. The corridor can remain a corridor for another minute.

NARRATOR: Outside the window, city buses are still taking people home. The ordinary world looks strange enough when everyone stops guessing what other people must have meant.

[Soft door chime, then silence. Credits identify the fiction and the source concepts behind it.]
