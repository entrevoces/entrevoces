[Clinical simulation. There is no real patient information. A careful interviewer and a patient actor speak over low room ambience.]

PATIENT: I have not had chest pain today. Yesterday I felt pressure, but I am not sure what caused it.

INTERPRETER (altered practice line): Hoy he tenido dolor de pecho. Ayer tuve dolor agudo por el medicamento.

[Silence. The clinician prepares to write a note; the supervising interpreter stops the exercise.]

SUPERVISOR: We heard three new facts that the patient did not give. Today changed from no chest pain to chest pain. Pressure became sharp pain. Uncertainty about cause became a claim about medication.

TRAINEE: It sounded like the same story because most words were familiar. I was listening for medical vocabulary rather than relationships.

PATIENT: What does “not sure” sound like? I do not want someone else to sound more certain than me.

SUPERVISOR: Preserve the uncertainty. If you miss a sound, disclose the difficulty and ask for repetition instead of making a clinically significant guess.

[The same actors repeat with a corrected version, leaving the hedging intact.]

INTERPRETER (corrected practice line): Hoy no he tenido dolor de pecho. Ayer sentí presión, pero no estoy seguro de qué la causó.

TRAINEE: I hear the opposite of the original error now. The small “no” carries a big difference.

SUPERVISOR: Exactly. The lesson is not a claim that every symptom means the same condition. It is the accuracy of what a person actually reported.

[The ambient room sound gives way to a soft bell; the scene ends before any diagnosis is invented.]
