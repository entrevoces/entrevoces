[SCENE ONE — 7:06 A.M. A bus shelter. Rain ticks on metal. Someone struggles to close a broken umbrella.]

NARRATOR: By seven in the morning, Teresa has already heard four different versions of the truth. None of them were told in the same language. The first came from a voice note sent to the wrong neighborhood group.

LUPE: Attention, residents. At nine, a person from the county will be at the building. The message says the court has made its decision. Repeat: made its decision. I am wearing lipstick suitable for an emergency.

TERESA: Lupe, who sent that?

LUPE: An anonymous woman who says her cousin knows a clerk. You cannot get more official than that without a seal.

TERESA: You can. You can get an actual notice. Also, your voice note says “the court has made its decision,” but the screenshot says “a hearing has been scheduled.”

LUPE: A small difference.

TERESA: A different legal event. A hearing is a chance for people to be heard. A decision is a result. We can’t turn one into the other because the group chat is impatient.

LUPE: Fine. I will post a correction.

[A rapid cascade of message notification tones.]

LUPE: The correction now has seventeen reactions, including a pineapple. Someone has asked whether this means they should bring a lawyer, a translator, or chairs.

TERESA: I can tell them where the court explains interpreter access. I can’t tell them what legal decision to make. And I haven’t seen the full notice.

[Bus air brakes. Doors open.]

NARRATOR: Teresa boards a bus carrying two folders, one sandwich, and a professional rule that she has come to fear more than a difficult word: never improve someone else’s story.

[SCENE TWO — 9:40 A.M. An interpreter training room. Chairs scrape. A clock ticks.]

TRAINER: Today's simulation is not about being eloquent. It is about being accurate under social pressure. You are not the witness. You are not the judge. You are the interpreter.

ACTOR ONE: I think I saw a man at the entrance. I might recognize his jacket. I couldn't tell whether he was carrying anything.

ACTOR TWO, AS NOVICE INTERPRETER: Vi al hombre en la entrada. Reconozco su chaqueta. Llevaba algo.

[The room goes quiet.]

TRAINER: Teresa, what happened?

TERESA: The interpretation upgraded three uncertainties into facts. “I think” disappeared. “Might recognize” became “recognize.” “Couldn't tell whether” became “was carrying.”

ACTOR TWO: It sounded awkward with so many maybes.

TERESA: The witness sounded uncertain. That awkwardness belongs to the witness. We can't polish it away.

TRAINER: Exactly. Your job is not to make testimony persuasive. Try again and keep the degree of certainty.

ACTOR TWO: Creo que vi a un hombre en la entrada. Tal vez reconocería su chaqueta. No pude distinguir si llevaba algo.

TRAINER: Better. We can discuss regional phrasing, but the uncertainty and limits of observation survived. Now a medical conversation. New actors.

ACTOR THREE, AS CLINICIAN: Did the pressure start before you took the medicine or after?

ACTOR FOUR, AS PATIENT: It started before. I took the medicine because I felt that pressure. I don't know if it helped.

ACTOR TWO, AS NOVICE: Empezó después de tomar la medicina. La tomó y se sintió mejor.

TERESA: That is a different history. We changed the sequence and invented an improvement.

ACTOR TWO: I heard “took the medicine” and “pressure.”

TERESA: Hearing the nouns wasn't enough. “Before” carried the timeline; “I don't know” carried the uncertainty. The big words weren't the dangerous part.

[Someone taps the desk twice, like a tiny gavel.]

TRAINER: A useful principle: if you didn't understand something, make the interpreting difficulty transparent and request clarification. Don't quietly fill the gap.

[SCENE THREE — 12:25 P.M. A café. Cups clink. A small radio plays a scratchy bolero.]

LUPE: I have a question. If the words are so fragile, why do people speak so fast?

TERESA: Because they don't know they're entering someone else's future.

LUPE: Very dramatic. You should write telenovelas.

MARTA: I brought the actual notice. I want help understanding what it says, not deciding what to do. Can you read the title aloud?

TERESA: “Notice of hearing.” It names a date and a place. That doesn't tell us what the outcome will be. For the specific legal meaning and your options, ask the court or a qualified legal advisor. The court should be able to explain how to request language assistance.

MARTA: Then the anonymous message that said I already lost my home was wrong?

TERESA: I can't establish the legal status from a group rumor. I can say that the screenshot was misquoted. The document says “hearing,” not “judgment.”

LUPE: Which means we need to apologize to everyone I told to pack their lamps.

MARTA: Especially the woman who already sold her lamp.

[A laugh, then the radio cuts out.]

[SCENE FOUR — 6:10 P.M. Teresa's kitchen. A phone vibrates on the table.]

NARRATOR: At home, Teresa takes off her shoes and plays the training recording one more time. She has missed a different little word this time. The patient said the pressure started *before*. The trainee said *after*. Without that one bridge between events, the story crosses to the wrong bank.

LUPE [VOICE NOTE]: I have fixed the post. I wrote: “A hearing was scheduled. No decision has been confirmed in the information we saw.” I also removed the pineapple.

TERESA: You finally kept the limits of what you know.

LUPE [VOICE NOTE]: I am now a woman of great restraint. But a new message has arrived. Someone says the notice was for apartment nine. Marta lives in six.

TERESA: Don't forward anything. First verify the apartment number and the document.

[Silence. Then an incoming message chime.]

UNKNOWN VOICE [VOICE NOTE]: Hello, Teresa. I'm calling from the clinic simulation. I need to correct something I said this morning. The person didn't say “I never had pain.” They said “I haven't had pain today.” I think someone removed the word *today* in the report.

NARRATOR: Teresa looks at the empty space beside her notebook where she has written three words: who, when, maybe. A new fourth word joins them: *today*.

[The radio starts again, playing the same melody in a different key.]

TERESA: Lupe, I'm going to need you to stop calling every message a verdict.

LUPE [VOICE NOTE]: I already changed the group name.

TERESA: To what?

LUPE: “Solo Avisos — Sujeto a Verificación.”

NARRATOR: For once, the group is quiet. Then the typing dots appear. Five people, all at once.

[END EPISODE — THE MISSING DAY]
