[Fictional court interpreter training scenario. Chair creaks. A clock ticks once, then disappears beneath the voice.]

WITNESS: I think I might have seen a red truck near the intersection. I cannot say whose it was.

INTERPRETER (altered training version): Vi la camioneta roja del acusado cerca de la intersección.

[A few listeners might miss the shift; the words “red truck” are familiar, but the identity and the certainty are not the same.]

TRAINER: Let's imagine the difference between “might” and “did,” then the difference between “cannot say whose” and “belonging to the defendant.”

TRAINEE: The interpreter made the witness an eyewitness of ownership when the witness said neither ownership nor certainty.

WITNESS: I don't want a stronger statement attributed to me. I said what I thought I saw.

TRAINER: You can use plain words, but do not strengthen an uncertain account. Accuracy includes preserving qualifications and limitations.

INTERPRETER (corrected practice version): Creo que tal vez vi una camioneta roja cerca de la intersección. No puedo decir de quién era.

TRAINEE: That sounds less dramatic, but it's closer to the truth of what the witness said.

TRAINER: A good interpreter doesn't decide how dramatic a fact should be.

[A quiet beat. The trainer asks a second question: what if the witness changes their mind? The trainee answers that the interpreter carries the new statement as a new statement, not an opportunity to rewrite the first. The record should show what each person said at the time. No one in the training room decides which memory is correct.]

[Short pause, no verdict. In the final replay the audience hears the source and altered rendering again, with the key words unchanged except for their missing qualifiers.]
