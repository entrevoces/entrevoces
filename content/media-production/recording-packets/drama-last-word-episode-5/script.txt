[EPISODE: Before the Signature. Scene 1 — A bus stop near an Oregon clinic. Distant rain, bus brakes, a phone notification.]

NARRATOR: Teresa's training exam was tomorrow. She had rehearsed chest pains, procedures, consultations and every word that sounded like a different word. But one sentence, written in tiny print, kept bothering her: “You may ask.”

LUPE: You may ask for a bigger coffee, but the cafe may charge you for it. Permission is not a promise.

TERESA: You have managed to explain modality and budgeting at the same time.

LUPE: My methods are not approved by any university.

[They enter a supervised simulation room. An actor is playing a patient, another actor is playing a clinician.]

CLINICIAN: Before you decide whether to sign the consent form, I can explain the options and answer any questions.

PATIENT: I'm not sure I understand the difference between what will happen and what might happen.

NOVICE INTERPRETER: Tiene que firmar para que le expliquen el procedimiento.

[The supervisor signals pause. It is not a rebuke; it is an invitation to examine the information.]

SUPERVISOR: What was the speaker offering? And what did the interpreter add?

TERESA: The clinician offered information before a choice. The interpreted sentence made signing a requirement before the explanation. It reversed the order and changed the patient's options.

PATIENT: I was asking about what might happen. “Might” did not mean “will.”

SUPERVISOR: Good. Let the original uncertainty stay uncertain. If an interpreter needs clarification, that can be handled transparently, without a guess.

[Scene 2 — Lunch outside. The wind steals Lupe's napkin, which makes three dignified attempts to escape.]

LUPE: I have a question. If the interpreter knows the answer, why can't she just explain the medicine herself?

TERESA: Because the professional explaining it has the clinical responsibility. The interpreter carries the exchange accurately and can ask for clarification when needed.

LUPE: So the interpreter is not a human search engine with a stethoscope.

TERESA: Correct, and I would be terrible at hearing heartbeats. My ears are full of commas.

[Scene 3 — Afternoon practice. The actors return with a new statement.]

CLINICIAN: The instructions say one tablet every other day. Please check with your team before changing the dose.

PATIENT: Does that mean a tablet today, none tomorrow, and one the next day?

TERESA (interpreting the patient's question): ¿Eso significa una tableta hoy, ninguna mañana y otra pasado mañana?

SUPERVISOR: That preserves the question. It doesn't answer on behalf of the clinician.

CLINICIAN: That's what the schedule says in this training example. For an actual prescription, the patient should follow the clinician's individualized instructions.

[Scene 4 — Evening, apartment elevator. A new notice is taped crookedly, with no name or date.]

LUPE: Oh no. Another mysterious paper. It says “final notice.”

TERESA: Final notice of what? Who sent it? What authority does it have? A dramatic label is not the full story.

LUPE: I was hoping this episode would end with dessert.

NARRATOR: They walk past the elevator without concluding anything. By now Teresa knows that a sentence can be dramatic and still be incomplete.

[Scene 5 — Teresa's apartment, later. A page of notes sits beside a bowl of oranges.]

TERESA: In this training recording, I changed a permission into an obligation. I didn't even notice until I replayed it.

LUPE: You have been replaying the same twelve seconds for an hour. The oranges are getting nervous.

TERESA: It sounds different now. What was offered was an explanation before a decision. What I heard after the mistranslation was a command.

RAFA: I used to think a confident voice made a sentence correct. Then I watched Lupe explain the dishwasher instructions with total confidence and flood the kitchen.

LUPE: Those instructions were unclear. Also, the dishwasher was very dramatic.

TERESA: Confidence isn't evidence. Listening closely and asking when necessary is the work.

[Music softens. She closes the notebook instead of adding another answer. For once the room is quiet.]

[Outro: warm guitar and one tiny bell. NEXT EPISODE: The Red Truck.]
