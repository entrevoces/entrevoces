[PODCAST: Radio La Cosa Exacta. Episode: The One-Word Alibi. Sound of an old radio dialing into a warm modern acoustic theme.]

HOST ANA: Welcome to La Cosa Exacta, where a tiny word can change a very big afternoon. I'm Ana, and I have a confession: I once thought “maybe” was a word people used when they didn't know how to finish a sentence.

GUEST ELENA: And now?

ANA: Now I know it might be the most important word in that sentence. Today we're listening to made-up interpreting practice cases about uncertainty, attribution, permission and time. They are stories, not legal or medical advice.

ELENA: Imagine a witness says, “My neighbor told me the car was red.” What facts do we have?

ANA: A neighbor said something. We haven't established what the witness saw.

ELENA: Right. Now imagine the rendering says, “I saw a red car.” The color stayed. The source of knowledge didn't.

[Sound of a bicycle bell. A listener sends in a voice note played by an actor.]

LISTENER PILAR: I keep catching the wrong big words. But the mistakes that change the meaning aren't always big words. They seem invisible.

ANA: A good observation. How can someone practice listening for invisible words?

ELENA: Try listening for a handful of relationships: who did it, what did they know, when did it happen, and how certain are they? Then hear the meaning again after a pause.

ANA: We have a little game. I'll read two statements. You can stop the recording if you hear a change, but there is no magic score for being quick. The point is the explanation afterward. First: “I have not had chest pain today.” Second: “I have had chest pain today.”

[Two beats of silence.]

ELENA: That's the negation. Keep the sound of the original separate from what we wish the sentence meant. If this were a real clinical encounter, the clinician would handle diagnosis and management, not the interpreter.

ANA: Next: “Return in five days if the fever continues.” Then: “Return in fifteen days when the fever continues.”

ELENA: Both the number and the condition changed. That is why a model judging vocabulary similarity alone would miss critical distinctions.

ANA: Our last caller says her family argues about “every other day” and “twice daily.” Can one be the same as the other?

ELENA: No. They are not automatically equivalent. One is an interval; the other is frequency per day. Any real medical instructions need confirmation with the treating care team.

[Sound bridge: cup set gently on saucer.]

ANA: Listeners, we have a surprise for you. My aunt Lupe has joined by telephone and insists she understands the exact meaning of every legal term.

LUPE: I understand that “hearing” means someone listens. On family calls, everyone hears but no one listens.

ELENA: That's very funny, and also the beginning of a useful distinction. A hearing is a proceeding, while a judgment is a court decision. We can't infer a decision from a scheduled hearing.

ANA: Lupe, are you giving legal advice?

LUPE: Absolutely not. I'm giving culinary advice. If someone says “simmer for ten minutes,” don't translate it as “boil for one hour.”

ELENA: The principle would apply: preserve timing and action. But we should still distinguish cooking from legal practice.

[Two seconds of friendly laughter.]

ANA: Before we leave, tell us what the best kind of interpreter training feels like.

ELENA: It sounds like a conversation worth listening to, with a mistake you can notice, a repair you can explain and a second opportunity where the meaning stays intact. Sometimes it is a story. Sometimes a song. Sometimes the correct response is simply: “Could you repeat that?”

ANA: That is our show. We will be back next week with an elevator, a file, and a woman who insists the truth can be filed under miscellaneous.

[Outro music; credits specify the actors and that the original practice cases are fictional.]
