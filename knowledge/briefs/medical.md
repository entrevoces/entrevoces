Build a substantial practitioner handbook for medical interpreters, not an Oregon credential guide and not an alphabetical medical dictionary. Audience: developing and working interpreters who must understand the encounter, recognize meaningful distinctions and carry meaning accurately. Scope should follow frequent and consequential interpreting work across intake, history, examination, diagnostic reasoning and uncertainty, common body systems and conditions, tests/imaging/laboratory language, treatment and procedures, medicines/routes/doses/regimens, consent and shared decision making, pain, emergency care, mental health, sexual/reproductive health, chronic care, discharge and follow-up. Include high-value anatomy/physiology, symptom/sign/diagnosis distinctions, quantities/units/negation/time, risk/probability, euphemism/register, sensitive language, abbreviations and false friends as the evidence supports them.

The knowledge agent must choose and justify a coherent professional coverage taxonomy, gather primary authoritative public sources, actually read them, and repeat acquisition until every important responsibility is grounded. Prefer credible clinical patient-education sources and professional interpreting standards; language glossaries supplement contextual explanations rather than replacing them. Include terms in real workflows, conceptual models, consequential near-neighbor distinctions, exceptions, labeled synthetic interpreting examples and appropriate clarification strategies. Never prescribe, diagnose, silently simplify a clinician's meaning or turn uncertain translations into authoritative equivalents. Label jurisdiction-specific rules and language variants explicitly; do not center Oregon or any single credential exam. Explain when a concept or institution lacks a direct counterpart. Cover scope, limitations and source currency honestly. Chapters need substantive integrated teaching, not summaries padded to an arbitrary word count. Significant important-topic gaps block a comprehensive-book claim and trigger more research.
